# Generative Tools in Neighborhood Bakeries

## Introduction

Neighborhood bakeries operate on thin margins and short planning horizons. Owners usually write orders on paper, estimate ingredient needs by memory and answer the phone while serving customers at the counter. Recent AI tools promise to reduce this load, especially for writing product descriptions, drafting promotional messages and summarizing weekly sales. Yet little is known about how such tools fit into the everyday routine of a very small food business.

This paper reports a six-month observation of four bakeries in a mid-sized city. Each shop received access to a text assistant and a simple agent that could read a spreadsheet of orders. The team recorded which tasks the owners delegated, how long each task took before and after the change, and where the outputs needed correction.

## Method

The observation combined weekly photographs of the order sheets, short diaries kept by the owners and a final group meeting. Tasks were coded into three groups: writing, planning and customer contact. The coding was done by two researchers, who compared their results and resolved disagreements by discussion.

## Results

Writing tasks benefited the most. Product descriptions that once took twenty minutes were drafted in under five, although the owners always edited the wording to match the brand. Planning tasks were more mixed. The agent produced reasonable production lists when the recipes were stored in a clear format, but it confused similar products when the spreadsheet used inconsistent names.

Customer contact tasks showed the largest risk. Automated replies sometimes promised delivery dates that the bakeries could not meet. After the owners added a rule that every promise must be checked against the production calendar, these errors almost disappeared.

## Discussion

The findings suggest that agents are most useful when the tasks are narrow, the data is tidy and a person keeps the final decision. Small bakeries do not need complex systems. They need a few reliable routines and a habit of reviewing what the tools produce.
