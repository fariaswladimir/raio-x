# Small Accounting Offices and Agent-Based Automation

## Introduction

Small accounting offices handle many repetitive tasks every month. They collect receipts, reconcile bank statements, prepare payroll summaries and remind clients about deadlines. Agent-based automation, in which software takes a sequence of actions toward a goal, is now available at prices that small offices can afford. However, accounting work involves confidential data, so the choice of tools carries legal and ethical weight as well as technical ones.

This case study follows three offices that tested agents for a single process each over one year. The offices kept control of their clients' files and used the agents only to draft outputs that a professional would then review and sign.

## Method

The case study used logs of completed tasks, error reports and interviews with the partners. Each office defined its own list of tasks before the test, so that the comparison focused on the change in workload rather than on the choice of software. The logs recorded the time spent on each task, the number of corrections and the reason for every correction.

## Results

Agents reduced the time spent on reconciliation tasks by about forty percent. The largest gains came from routine matching of payments to invoices. Tasks that required reading unusual contracts or interpreting tax rules still needed a professional from start to finish, and the agents often missed exceptions in these cases.

Privacy controls shaped adoption more than cost. Two offices chose tools that process files on their own servers, even though these options were more expensive. The third office accepted a cloud service after reading its data policy, and it limited the agent to anonymized copies of the records.

## Discussion

The case suggests that agents are a sensible choice for small offices when the tasks are well defined and the review step is protected. The cost of a mistake in accounting is high, so the time saved must be weighed against the time needed to check the results. The offices that planned this balance from the start reported the most stable outcomes.
